Calling Card Tiles

FILE 1Try it

One banner, four cells, ten edges

A calling card is a 4:1 banner. On a unit grid it covers a strip of 1×4 cells: a bar. That simple shape has more going on than a square tile.

Four real rotations

Turning the bar 90° stands it upright. Turning it 180° keeps the footprint but reverses the order of its cells. When the four cells carry different emblems, all four rotations are different moves.

Ten edge segments

Two long sides of 4 segments and two ends of 1. Laid flat in a stack it touches 4 tiles. In a running bond (each course offset by 2 cells) it touches 6. Stood upright across four courses it can touch 10, one per segment.

Rotation changes connectivity

A flat tile is a quiet brick. An upright tile is a hub that ties four courses together. So choosing a rotation is a strategy call, not just a fitting problem.

Packing is a design dial

1×4 bars can fill an a×b board perfectly only if 4 divides a or b. So 8×8 and 8×9 can be filled; 7×7 and 9×9 never can. A solitaire wants a fillable board. A versus game wants leftovers to fight over.

Art reads across tiles

Two flat tiles end to end make an 8:1 strip, four make a 16:1 panorama. Four stacked make a square picture. Upright tiles show their art sideways; inspecting a tile always shows it upright and full size.

Phones fit it

A portrait iPhone is about 390pt wide. An 8-column board gives 48pt cells and 192×48pt tiles, which are easy to tap. Ten columns is the practical limit.

Diamond Disc Peak Cross Shape and colour both differ, so the emblems stay readable for colour-blind players.

FILE 2Pillar

A big collection should feel exciting, never pay-to-win

Every owned card becomes a tile skin. A bigger collection gives variety, sets to complete, foil tiles and a wall to show off. Gameplay values never come from the art: each card's emblems come from a fixed, balanced table of 16 patterns, so a starter player has every pattern in the game.

Fairness

  • A starter set of 48 original cards (12 series of 4, 3 panorama sets) covers every pattern. A player who never links has the whole game.
  • Crosswise and any ranked mode use a neutral pool; your collection only skins your side.
  • The daily leaderboard ranks the base score. Set bonus stars go on a separate, personal track. Matchmaking ignores collection size.

The loop with Call of Duty (hypothetical account link)

  1. A player unlocks a calling card in Call of Duty: a challenge, mastery, battle pass or event.
  2. It shows up here as a new tile the next time the hypothetical link syncs.
  3. It opens a set objective, a mastery track or a daily bonus.
  4. The set screen says "2 of 4". Missing cards show as silhouettes with their unlock hint.
  5. The player goes back to Call of Duty with a concrete card to chase.

Part 2 · 7 Nov 2026 · task cct-depth

Deeper concepts

The head asked for "a little more depth, even with simple rules". Part 2 adds four concepts with more decisions per turn. They are inspired by the genres of Neuroshima Hex, Might and Magic: Clash of Heroes and Puzzle Quest. Nothing is copied: no names, factions, unit lists, board shapes, icons, terms or art. Each file says what is borrowed as a genre convention and what is new.

New recommendation: Banner Front first. Muster is the backup, and Running Bond becomes a later daily mode.

FILE APlayable · Recommended

Banner Front

Tile-placement battle · solo vs AI or pass-and-play · 8–10 min

"Every calling card is a squad four cells long. Guns on one side, a shield on the other. Lay them down, turn them right, and let initiative decide who fires first."

  1. Draw up to 3 tiles.
  2. Play 2: place units, turned 4 ways.
  3. Discard the third.
  4. Battles resolve by initiative 3 → 0.

Board and Command

An 8 wide × 7 deep grid. Each side starts with a Command banner in the middle of its back row: Blades on all 10 edges, initiative 0, 10 HP. The first player's Command has 11 HP and plays 2 tiles on turn 1 too: the first-player fix.

Edge icons

  • Gun: fires straight out and hits the first tile in line. A friendly tile blocks it.
  • Heavy Gun: a Gun that deals 2.
  • Blade: hits the enemy tile touching that edge. Ignores Shields.
  • Shield: stops Guns arriving at that edge.

Each attacker hits each enemy tile at most once per step, with its best hit. A long side spreads damage; an end focuses it.

Battles and winning

A battle happens when someone plays a Battle order, when no free 4-cell line is left, and once more when both decks are empty. Within a step everyone strikes at once; the dead leave before the next step. Destroy the enemy Command, or have more Command HP after the final battle. A tie goes to more unit HP left.

Decision tensions

  • Tempo vs exposure: a Battle order cashes in now, before the reply; a board-fill battle comes after it.
  • Facing: a long side covers 4 lines but shows a 4-segment target; end-on is small and shielded but fires one line.
  • Initiative races: Scouts at 3 kill Rifles at 2 before they shoot.
  • Lines and blocking: your own tiles block your Guns; an upright tile cuts four enemy lanes.
  • Board-fill timing and the discard: every turn you give up one of 3.

Borrowed vs new

Borrowed: draw 3 / play 2 / discard 1, a shared board, units that never move, initiative-stepped battles, a battle trigger tile, a base.

New: 1×4 units on a square grid, 10-segment edge icons, one hit per target (spread vs focus), friendly line blocking, 4-way rotation of asymmetric bars.

What more cards add

High

Your cards skin your unit types; your favourite card is your Command banner, big and inspectable by the opponent. Series sets unlock army banners; foil cards animate when their unit strikes. Ranked uses the neutral 20-tile deck for everyone.

Risks

  • Readability of 10 icons per tile on a phone. The battle preview overlay is essential and doubles as the tutorial.
  • A Command with Blades on all 10 edges may be strong; test long sides only.
  • The genre family is well known, so the originality bar is real.

The aha
Turning one unit upright cuts four enemy lanes and opens four of yours, and your Scouts at initiative 3 kill their Rifles before they ever fire.

FILE BTry a turn · Backup

Muster

Formation-puzzle combat · 15–20 min (too long yet)

"Line up your troops into charges and walls. Your calling-card elite turns the battle: stand it up as a lance, or lay it down as a bulwark."

  1. Spend 3 actions on your 6×6 side.
  2. Stack 3 of a colour: a Charge.
  3. Line 3 side by side: Walls.
  4. Charges fire at the enemy hero.

The elite: Lance or Bulwark

The 4:1 calling card is your elite. Upright, as a Lance, it fills 4 cells at the back of one column; a troop of its colour landing behind it makes a power 8 Charge that fires in 3 turns. Flat, as a Bulwark, it puts 3 HP walls across the front of 4 columns at once. The planner AI split its elites 1:1; the greedy AI chose Bulwark 3:1.

Decision tensions

  • The same troops are either a Charge or a Wall.
  • Charges land later, so you aim at where the enemy will be weak, and wall where theirs will land.
  • A Remove that causes a formation, or 2 formations at once, gives +1 action.
  • Call timing: refilling costs an action.

Borrowed vs new

Borrowed: per-player column grids, vertical attack formations with countdowns, horizontal walls, removal chains, reinforcement calls, an action budget.

New: the 1×4 elite with a rotation choice, a lance triggered by a matching troop behind it, one wall per column with stacking HP, charges jumping idle troops.

What more cards add

Medium

Elites are your calling cards (2 per battle, neutral rules). A full series unlocks a troop theme. Foil elites get an animated charge-up.

Risks

  • Long fights: about half the games reach the turn cap.
  • Closest of the four to its genre family.
  • Many small pieces on a phone: stack the two sides in portrait.

FILE CStoryboard

Spell Rail

Match battle with abilities · 6–10 min per fight

"A shared match board, and your calling cards are mounted on its edge. Match next to your banners to charge them, and next to theirs to deny them."

  1. Mount 4 ability cards on the rail.
  2. Swap to match 3 or more.
  3. Matches charge the cards beside them.
  4. Cast: Sweep, Convert, Drain, Bulwark.

Rotation and orientation

A card on the rail borders exactly 4 cells, its own length. Top and bottom rails hold cards flat and read row matches; side rails hold them upright and read columns. Sweep clears the line it borders, so the slot decides what it can destroy. A lighter use of the shape than A or B.

Decision tensions

  • Denial: each match shapes the board your opponent sees next.
  • Rail geography: feed your cards, starve theirs. Mounting order is a mini-draft.
  • Mana vs charge, and extra turns from lines of 4 and cascades.

Borrowed vs new

Borrowed: a shared swap-to-match grid, mana colours, a damage emblem, mana-cost abilities, extra turns, RPG levelling.

New: ability cards on 4-cell rail slots charged by adjacent matches, slot drafting, rail orientation.

What more cards add

Very high

Your cards are your spellbook's faces. A full series mounted together gives an aura. Campaign bounties ask for a card from a series you own, always with a starter-set alternative.

Risks

  • Swap-match genre fatigue and cascade luck.
  • The weakest 4:1 hook of the four. Not simulated this round.

FILE DStoryboard

Banner Front Campaign

Tile battle with a roguelite army draft · 40–60 min run, 8 min per battle

"Banner Front's battles, strung into a solo campaign where your army deck grows between fights."

  1. Start with a 12-tile army.
  2. Fight a commander with a special rule.
  3. Win, draft 1 of 3 new tiles.
  4. Trim the army at camps; 8 battles.

Depth

Deck-shaping on top of Banner Front's tactics, plus enemy rules that ask for different facings. New tile types add icons on new edge layouts.

What more cards add

High

Unlocked cards become the faces of drafted tiles. Series sets unlock starting army themes. The run history is a gallery of the cards that carried you.

Risks and verdict

Heavy content load: enemy rules and new tiles. It comes after Banner Front versus is proven. The best long-term shape, and the solo story for Banner Front, not the first prototype.

FILE 3.1Playable · Daily mode

Running Bond

Tile-laying solitaire with a daily variant · 8–10 min

"Build a wall out of your calling cards. Bridge every joint, match the emblems, and finish the course."

  1. Pick 1 of 3 tiles from the market.
  2. Rotate it 4 ways.
  3. Lay it in the 8×8 wall, under gravity.
  4. Score, then repeat 16 times.

Rotation and orientation

  • Flat tiles are cheap bricks. Upright tiles are hubs that touch up to 10 neighbours but leave tall gaps.
  • A 180° turn reverses the emblem order, which often turns +0 into +2.
  • The wall never rotates, so it reads the same in portrait and landscape.

What more cards add

Very high
  • The finished wall is a gallery of your cards: the Wall of Fame.
  • Series objectives like "3 Sniper Mastery cards in one course: +4 stars" point straight at unlocks.
  • Foil tiles shimmer, and panorama sets line up across a course.

Risks

  • It can read as falling blocks without the falling. Answer: a slower, planned feel and no line clears.
  • Score math must stay legible, so every scoring edge pops its own number.
  • The "mason's insert" (sliding a tile under an overhang) is physically odd and needs a player test.

The aha
A tile stood upright in a gap ties four courses together and scores 5 or more contacts at once. Then you notice that greedy scoring leaves holes, and a perfect wall needs planning.

Pivot

Daily rotation puzzle · 2–5 min

"Swing the banners. Get your card to the exit."

  1. Read a 7×7 board of about 6 bars.
  2. Slide any bar 1 cell along its length…
  3. …or swing it 90° around either end.
  4. Get the gold key bar onto the exit.

Rotation and orientation

  • The whole puzzle is rotation. The swing's swept wedge is what makes it hard.
  • The board is square, so portrait and landscape look identical.

What more cards add

Medium
  • The key bar is your featured card; all bars are skinned from your collection.
  • "Series week": owning that week's series unlocks a bonus 4th-star puzzle. An extra puzzle, not an easier one.

Risks

  • Needs a generator plus solver; the solver exists, quality filtering takes time.
  • The wedge must be shown: a preview on press plus the inspect view.
  • Steer clear of the traffic-puzzle "car to the exit" framing and its piece set.

The aha
"I have to swing that bar out of the way, which frees the corner, which lets mine swing round."

FILE 3.3Playable · 2 players

Crosswise

Versus, Landscape against Portrait · 5–8 min

"One of you can only lay banners across, the other only down. Hold the phone your way."

  1. Share a 9×8 board with 11 stars.
  2. Place bars in your direction: 1, then 2 per turn.
  3. Cover stars; block the other side.
  4. Most stars wins.

Rotation and orientation

  • Your orientation is your side.
  • In pass-and-play, Portrait holds the phone upright and Landscape turns it sideways, so each reads their own banners upright.
  • Board shape is the balance lever: wide boards favour Landscape, tall boards favour Portrait.

What more cards add

Medium-low
  • Gameplay uses a neutral pool, so the pull is pure flex: your banners and their mastery frames, for the opponent to inspect.

Risks

  • Can feel solved at small sizes; randomised star layouts per match are needed.
  • First-player advantage needs tuning, and solo play needs a good AI.

The aha
"One down-bar there kills three of their across-moves." A pure territory game, learned in 30 seconds.

FILE 3.4Storyboard

Broadside

Tactics · 15–20 min

"Every calling card is a warship 4 cells long. Fire from the long sides, ram with a swing."

  1. Field 4 ships on a 10×10 sea.
  2. Spend 2 actions per ship: advance or swing.
  3. Fire a broadside or a bow shot.
  4. Sink the flagship or 3 ships.

Rotation and orientation

Facing is everything. A long broadside also shows the enemy a long, hittable side. Turning end-on is safe, but your fire is weak.

What more cards add

Medium

Your fleet is skinned from your cards and series fleets fly a cosmetic flag. Unit types come from the neutral table.

Risks

  • Heavier rules, an AI cost, and the longest session.
  • Avoid hidden-grid guessing in the Battleship style.

The aha
"Swing to ram, and end the turn showing them only your 1-segment bow."

FILE 3.5Storyboard

Banner Keep

Deck-building roguelite · 25–40 min run, 5-min fights

"Your deck is your calling cards. Their edges are sockets. Place them so the effects chain."

  1. Draw 5, place 3 on a 6×6 keep.
  2. Each card fires its sides and its tip.
  3. Resolve chains into Guard and Power.
  4. Win, draft a card, move on the map.

Rotation and orientation

Rotation decides which neighbours a card triggers. The best turns rotate one card so it chains the whole board.

What more cards add

High

Your collection seeds the draft pool's skins and unlocks series relics (cosmetic plus objective). The pool of effects is the same for everyone.

Risks

  • A crowded genre that needs lots of content and balance.
  • Must stay clear of KillingCard's lanes and columns.
  • Biggest scope of the seven.

The aha
"Rotate the Spark card upright and its end effect fires the Doubler, which fires everything."

FILE 3.6Storyboard

Tilt Stack

Real-time stacking · 3-min rounds

"Banners fall wherever down is, and down is wherever you turn your phone."

  1. Banners fall into an 8×12 well.
  2. Rotate them in flight.
  3. Touch 3 of a series to cash in.
  4. Gravity turns; everything loose slides.

Rotation and orientation

Device orientation drives gravity, with an on-screen button for orientation lock and PC. Piece rotation is a must.

What more cards add

Medium

Series matching uses your cards. More series means more series colours, not power, which is not obviously exciting.

Risks

  • Highest trademark risk: no line clears, no tetromino set, no borrowed well proportions or terms.
  • Orientation lock and motion fatigue on phones.

Setting up a stack that cashes in only after the gravity turn.

FILE 3.7Storyboard

Panorama Patience

Solitaire · 5–8 min

"Deal the strips. Rebuild the pictures."

  1. Deal 16 strips into 4 columns.
  2. Move strips to rebuild each picture.
  3. Spend a token to turn a sideways strip.
  4. Complete all 4 pictures.

Rotation and orientation

Sideways strips are blocked until turned. In portrait a picture is a square column; in landscape the 4 squares sit in a row.

What more cards add

Low for existing cards, high for original panorama sets

It needs art authored as cut squares, so it can't use existing calling cards. It works better as a reward inside Running Bond.

Risks

  • Thin as a game: only 4 ranks per suit, close to classic patience.
  • Avoid Klondike-style layout and foundation rules.

Three questions for the head

Shown for reference only. Answer them on the Studio Floor board, on task cct-depth. Part 1's questions (task cct-ideas) are replaced by these: question 1 supersedes the earlier choice of first prototype.

1. Which first prototype?

  1. Banner Front: the most depth per rule, proven in simulation. Recommended
  2. Running Bond, as planned in Part 1. Cheapest, solitaire.
  3. Muster: formation combat, but long sessions until it is tuned.

2. First play mode for Banner Front?

  1. Solo against an AI with 3 difficulty levels, plus local pass-and-play. Recommended
  2. Online versus. Needs a backend.
  3. The roguelite campaign from the start.

3. Complexity budget for version 1?

  1. 5 unit types, 4 edge icons, no move or push tiles: the simulated version. Recommended
  2. Add Shift and Push tiles from day one.
  3. Asymmetric armies (different decks per side).
